### The Big Picture

Data Skaters is a hands-on learning experience that introduces students in Grades 5 - 12 to data literacy and data sovereignty through hockey. Students explore the Indigenous principles of OCAP® (Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession), and learn how data can be collected, analysed, and interpreted. The program focuses on skills in numeracy, coding, computational thinking, critical thinking, team work, collaboration, and AI literacy.

Data Skaters is flexible and adaptable to any schedule, space, or resource setup. Teachers can combine, simplify, or reorder activities as needed while maintaining core outcomes: data literacy, critical thinking, collaboration, and responsible data use.

## Set-Up

This works best in a large space such as a gymnasium, or outdoors if weather permits.

Students should be in groups of six or fewer. Each group will need:

* an internet-connected device with a camera, such as a laptop or tablet
* a hockey net (mini, full-size with part blocked off, or a target on the wall)
* a mini stick or full-size stick
* a foam ball or soft puck
* masking tape or painter's tape and a marker
* distance markings in front of the net at approximately 2 feet, 5 feet, 7 feet, and 10 feet
* measuring tape
  * (optional) vertical tape on the wall with markings at 10 cm increments for measuring height
  * (optional) horizontal tape on the wall with markings at 10 cm increments for measuring wingspan
* (optional) pulse oximeter or similar device for measuring heart rate
* (optional) paper and pencils for recording data before entering it into the digital forms

As well, you will need:

* reliable internet access
  * ensure that the [Data Skaters Website](https://datadunkers.ca/skaters) is not blocked, also test that [card creator](https://datadunkers.ca/skaters/card-creator/index.html?testing=true) renders correctly
* projector or screen for displaying slides and data visualizations
* supplies for creating player cards
  * colour printer
  * scissors or paper cutters
  * card protectors (hard plastic or soft)
  * tables for assembling and displaying the cards
* (optional) for esports data collection:
  * game consoles, laptops, or computer lab with [Rocket League](https://www.rocketleague.com) installed
  * gaming controllers (two per station for 1v1 games)
  * [Epic Games](https://store.epicgames.com) accounts logged in
* signed media consent forms if you are sharing photos of the event

**Important**: Decide on an *event key* (e.g. MFNSS) and input it into each of the laptops to ensure that all of the collected data is going to the same database.

## Introduction

### Player Card

Starting with the end in mind, show students a [player card](https://datadunkers.ca/skaters/card-creator/index.html?demo=true) as an example of how their data will be used.

* They will be taking their own photo and creating a four-character player ID from their actual initials and two digits. Although the forms call it a nickname, the initials must be the student's real initials for reliable error checking.
* The card will include the data they collect about their traits and shot accuracy.
* There will also be visualizations on the back about how their archetype was determined, and correlations in some of the data that will be collected today.
  * The player archetype is a data-informed profile that describes a pattern in a student's results.
  * The archetypes are Sniper, Speed Winger, Playmaker, Defenseman, Power Forward, and Two-Way Forward.

### Overview of the Day

1. Introduce the supporters, the purpose of the day, and how data science, data sovereignty, and artificial intelligence are related.
2. Take a player photo at their data collection station (hockey net) using the [photo booth](https://datadunkers.ca/skaters/photobooth).
3. Record their [traits](https://datadunkers.ca/skaters/traits) (measurements) such as height, wingspan, resting heart rate, and reaction time.
4. Discuss data sovereignty and data science.
5. Take [shots](https://datadunkers.ca/skaters/shots) on a hockey net from four different distances and record their success rate.
6. (Optional) Play a round of Rocket League and record their individual [esports results](https://datadunkers.ca/skaters/rocketleague) such as goals and assists.
7. Visualize and analyse the group data and their own data.
8. Play at least one game on "fair teams" as determined by AI from their collected data.
9. Discuss the archetypes and get a unique player card with their data.

### Math Connections

#### Data and Number Sense

* measurement of length, time, counting successful shots
* first-hand versus second-hand data: differentiate between data they collect themselves and NHL statistics or open data
* number operations (e.g. doubling chest to fingertip distance to find wingspan, multiplying beats in 15 seconds by four to get beats per minute for heart rate)
* converting fractions to percents (e.g. successful shots out of six converted to shot percentage)
* measures of central tendency (e.g. averaging three trials of reaction time, average shot percentages)

#### Graphing and Relationships

* comparing different types of data visualizations
* interpreting bar graphs, pie charts, scatter plots, correlation matrices, and radar plots
* how a trendline can show the correlation between two variables
* differentiating between positive and negative correlations, strong and weak correlations
* how outliers or errors in the data can affect averages
* predicting the likelihood of outcomes

#### Possible Extensions

* interpolating and extrapolating trends
* representing functional relationships algebraically
* standard deviation and z-scores
* regression analysis
* algorithmic and computational thinking

### Performance Mindset Mindfulness

*If you have other adult helpers, this may be a good time to have a facilitator talk with them about their roles during the data collection process.*

Introduce students to [Lotus 8 Esports Performance Mindset Mindfulness](https://lotus8esports.com/performance-mindset-mindfulness). These exercises are designed to settle the body, regulate breathing, bring attention to the present moment, and prepare students mentally for performance.

Have students sit comfortably on the ground or in a chair. If seated on the ground, students may use a relaxed Lotus or cross-legged position with their palms facing upward.
Ask students to close their eyes, if comfortable.

Guide them through relaxing the body:
Lower and soften the shoulders. Relax the hips, legs and feet. Bring attention to the face and relax the forehead, eyebrows and jaw.
Before beginning the count, ask students to slowly breathe in through their nose while silently saying "Breathing in calmness."

1. Relax the body: 

* Unclench the jaw and drop the tongue from the roof of the mouth.
* Drop the shoulders and sit tall.
* Feel the ground supporting you.

2. 3-Count Breath:

* Slowly inhale through the nose — 1… 2… 3.
* Gently hold for 2 seconds.
* Slowly exhale — 3… 2… 1.

3. Controlled head rolls:

* Drop your chin straight down to your chest to release the back of your neck.
* Slowly roll your right ear toward your right shoulder and hold it there for a breath.
* Do three half-circle rolls through the center between your shoulders.

4. 4-Count Breath:

* Slowly inhale — 1… 2… 3… 4.
* Gently hold for 2 seconds.
* Slowly exhale — 4… 3… 2… 1.

5. Wrist, Forearm, and Finger Mobility:

* Gently apply pressure with your palm on each finger of the opposite hand, one at a time. Breathe in and out with each gentle push.
* Extend both arms straight out in front of the chest at shoulder height with your fingers splayed out.
* Roll your wrists slowly out and down, then back up. Repeat three times.

6. 5-Count Breath:

* Slowly inhale — 1… 2… 3… 4… 5.
* Gently hold for 2 seconds.
* Slowly exhale — 5… 4… 3… 2… 1.

Explain that today will include successful attempts, missed shots, and moments of pressure. The goal is to remain present and focus on the next opportunity.

### OCAP® and Data sovereignty

Developed in 1998 by the First Nations Information Governance Centre (FNIGC), OCAP® is a set of foundational principles that establishes First Nations data sovereignty - the right of First Nations communities to own, control, access, and possess data collected about their people, culture, and lands.

In Data Skaters, OCAP® principles are directly integrated:

* Ownership: While gathering data to create player cards, students evaluate who holds the rights to an athlete's performance data, health metrics, and digital representation.
* Control: Students analyse who has the authority to decide how statistics are interpreted, categorized, and presented, such as when determining player archetypes.
* Access: Students discuss who should be granted permission to view detailed versus high-level player statistics, balancing transparency with privacy.
* Possession: Students consider where data is stored, who is responsible for keeping it secure, and what happens to it after the player cards have been generated.

The term [data sovereignty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_sovereignty) relates to a nation's regulations and control over data generated within its borders. It can be related to transnational data flow or the relationship of Indigenous groups to post-colonial states.

All of the data collected is either stored locally or on a Canadian server managed by the Data Skaters team. Participant data will not be retained.

## Data Collection

Each student should create a player ID using their actual initials and a two-digit number (e.g. PS43). Students must use the same ID all day. Although the forms call it a nickname, the initials must be the student's real initials so mistakes can be identified and corrected. Consider writing the ID on a piece of masking tape for students to use like a nametag.

Divide students into groups, and ensure they know their group number (e.g. tape label on the hockey net).

We recommend getting students actively collecting data as quickly as possible, with minimal preamble.

Using the [photo booth](https://datadunkers.ca/skaters/photobooth), students are introduced to the data collection process and using their nicknames.

### Traits Form

The [traits form](https://datadunkers.ca/skaters/traits) requires students to make various measurements and record their data.

*After this it would be good to gather the students back together to talk about data sovereignty and data science, and remind them of the purpose of the activities.*

### Shot Input Form

Students take shots on a hockey net from four different distances and record their success rate in the [shots form](https://datadunkers.ca/skaters/shots).

### Rocket League

If you are able to set up computers for students to play 1v1 Rocket League Snow Day, they can record their individual [esports results](https://datadunkers.ca/skaters/rocketleague).

#### Setup and Match Format

Set up each station with Rocket League launched:

* Exhibition Mode
* 1v1
* Snow Day
* 3-minute match length
* no bots

Connect two controllers per station so students can play head-to-head in split-screen mode.

Have pairs rotate through the esports stations (perhaps while other groups complete their shots or trait measurements). Once a match ends, both players remain on the post-game scoreboard screen to record their individual goals, assists, saves, and shots into the [esports results form](https://datadunkers.ca/skaters/rocketleague) using their four-character nickname.

## Data Analysis and Visualizations

Once the students have collected all of their data, the [visualizations page](https://datadunkers.ca/skaters/visualizations) will allow you to show them various data visualizations for their data. Show the page on the big screen, and walk through the charts together.

### Outliers

Ask students to spot obviously incorrect numbers (e.g. a wingspan that is half of a player's height). Emphasize that input accuracy matters, and that the forms were morstly designed to prevent the input of impossible values (e.g. resting heart rate of 5 bpm). Correcting incorrect values is called *data cleaning*, and is where data scientists spend significant amounts of time.

### Jupyter Notebook

Jupyter is a browser-based coding environment that lets students analyse data using Python without installing software or creating user accounts. In Data Skaters, students can use a notebook on JupyterLite, which doesn't require a server or login, to import their collected data and create visualizations using pre-written code.

Participants can run their own copy of a JupyterLite notebook by clicking on the [data analysis link](https://datadunkers.ca/jupyterlite/tree/?path=data-skaters%2Fdata-skaters.ipynb) on the Data Skaters page.

Guide students step-by-step through the pre-written cells to import data and generate summary statistics and custom charts. Encourage them to change variable names inside the plotting code to test their own hypotheses.

### AI Analysis

To begin analysing the data using AI, download the data file from the [collected data page](https://datadunkers.ca/skaters/collected).

Using a local AI model (preferred) or an online AI tool approved by your school, add the downloaded data file and use prompts such as:

* `Who is the tallest in the dataset?`
* `Who has the fastest reaction time?`
* `Who are the top 5 overall shooters across all distances combined?`
* `Which traits seem to be correlated?`
* `Create a scatterplot showing how wingspan relates to shooting performance.`
* `Does faster reaction time translate into more saves or higher scores in Rocket League?`
* `Rank the groups based on their shooting performance.`
* `Do skaters with lower resting heart rates maintain more consistent shooting accuracy as distance increases?`
* `Plot the average shots_made across distances.`
* `Do students born in earlier months have better performance?`
* `Compare right-handed, left-handed, and ambidextrous skaters across reaction time, total shots made, and Rocket League goals.`
* `How would a team of these students do when playing against the Winnipeg Jets?`

## AI-Mediated Team Creation

Continuing the AI analysis, give the tool a prompt such as:

`We would like to play some 4 vs 4 ball hockey scrimmage matches, generate fair teams based on the data.`

Then set up and play some matches, and discuss with students if they thought those were fair teams and why or why not. You can also talk about other factors that go into team play that were not collected in the data set (e.g. friendships, leadership skills, effort and attitude, communication and teamwork). This highlights the importance of "data-informed decision making", combining data analysis with human insight and judgment.

## Player Archetypes and Student Critique

Archetypes are generated by comparing each student's collected data to a set of predefined archetype profiles. This process shows how data can be used to identify patterns and group similar characteristics. Archetypes are based only on the data collected in these activities, and should be used for reflection and discussion rather than as a complete description of the student. Important qualities like leadership, effort, and teamwork are not captured in this data set.

For each player, the app calculates z-scores (how far a value is above or below the average) for shots at the different distances, plus the player’s height, wingspan, reaction time, and resting heart rate. It then scores the six archetypes:

* Sniper: accuracy at longer distances
* Speed Winger: accuracy at medium distances plus reaction time
* Playmaker: wingspan plus lowest personal accuracy percentage
* Defenseman: accuracy at short distance plus height
* Power Forward: accuracy at 2 feet and 5 feet plus height and wingspan
* Two-Way Forward: medium-distance accuracy and resting heart rate

## Data Ownership

Before distributing the printed player cards, remind students that they retain ownership, control, access, and possession of their data. It will only be on the card. After the event the data will be deleted from the Data Skaters server.

Possible discussion questions:

1. Who owns the data collected during Data Skaters, and who should decide how it is used?
2. How is collecting information about people similar to collecting information about Indigenous communities?
3. Why is control of your own data important?
4. Should students have the right to see, correct, or remove their data after it has been collected? Why or why not?
5. What are the benefits and risks of sharing data with others, including AI systems, to make decisions or predictions?
6. What important things about a person cannot be captured by data, and why should these missing pieces matter when making decisions?
7. How can these ideas affect how we use other digital systems?

## Differentiation and Accessibility

Differentiation and accessibility ensure that all students can engage with core data science concepts, regardless of their math background, reading level, or coding experience. By adjusting task complexity, leveraging visual sports analogies, and scaffolding the coding elements, teachers can ensure every learner can analyse data, understand player archetypes, and participate in evidence-based team building. For example:

|Need|Support|
|-|-|
|Physical|Offer seated or short-distance stations, recorder roles, larger targets, and low-movement alternatives|
|Data-entry|Use paired entry, printed tracking sheets, or adult helpers|
|Language|Post vocabulary with icons, model sentence stems, and allow oral responses before writing|
